# Reconstruction Arm IV

## Worship Repertoire, Performance, and Sonic Reconstruction

### Executive Purpose

Arm IV of the Calvary Spokane Reconstruction documents and reconstructs the worship-music environment in which I participated directly as a performer.

Unlike the sermon corpus or the childhood/youth curriculum reconstruction, this arm is grounded in surviving **primary physical artifacts from my own participation in the church's worship system**: paper notebooks containing chord sheets, lyrics, chord progressions, occasional artist references, annotations, and duplicate copies of songs used in worship contexts.

These notebooks can potentially reconstruct not merely what music existed around me, but the repertoire I was **given, expected to learn, rehearsed, and helped transmit to other people through live worship performance**.

The central research question is:

> **What theological, emotional, cultural, and sonic environment was I trained to embody and reproduce through worship while participating in Calvary Spokane's youth and congregational services?**

---

## Historical Participation Context

My participation included at least three distinct worship environments:

### High-School Youth Worship Team

I played on the worship team associated with the high-school youth group.

This repertoire belongs within the broader adolescent formation environment, but it also warrants independent treatment because I was no longer only receiving religious formation. I was participating in the production of that environment for other youth.

### Main Sanctuary — Sunday Services

I also performed on stage during Sunday services in the main sanctuary.

Material associated with this setting represents part of the church's primary congregational worship repertoire.

### “New Area” — Thursday Services

I additionally performed during Thursday services in the space then known as the **New Area**.

This should be treated as a distinct performance context rather than automatically merged with the Sunday repertoire.

The three environments may overlap substantially, but their repertoires, arrangements, audience, emotional tone, and theological emphases should remain independently reconstructable.

---

# Primary Artifact Corpus

The primary source material consists of several paper worship notebooks accumulated during this period.

The notebooks contain some combination of:

* lyrics;
* chord names;
* chord progressions;
* song titles;
* occasional artist or recording references;
* handwritten notes;
* arrangement markings;
* key information;
* performance annotations;
* duplicate chord sheets;
* and potentially other contextual clues.

The notebooks are currently scattered among my possessions and contain substantial duplication.

They have not yet been systematically inventoried or digitized.

Some songs were **given to me as part of the church worship repertoire**, while other material was **added by me independently**.

I expect that I will often be able to distinguish these categories, although the classification may require manual review.

This distinction is fundamental.

A song I personally added to a notebook does not automatically establish that Calvary Spokane supplied, endorsed, or performed it.

---

# What the Notebooks Can Establish

The notebooks should not automatically be interpreted as historical setlists.

A chord sheet can establish that a song was part of my working musical materials without establishing:

* the exact date I learned it;
* how many times I performed it;
* which service used it;
* whether I ever performed it publicly;
* the exact arrangement;
* or the precise recording on which the local version was based.

Arm IV therefore separates several evidence levels.

## Repertoire Evidence

The artifact establishes that a song was among the materials available to or assigned to me.

Where I can identify a sheet as church-provided, this becomes direct evidence that the song belonged to the repertoire I was expected to prepare or perform.

## Performance Evidence

Additional evidence establishes that I actually performed the song.

This may come from:

* personal memory;
* setlists;
* service recordings;
* photographs;
* videos;
* annotations;
* calendars;
* or other surviving records.

## Setlist Evidence

Stronger evidence establishes that a specific song was performed during a particular service or event.

This is the most precise category and may only be recoverable for a minority of performances.

These levels must remain distinct.

---

# Digitization Strategy

The physical notebooks should be preserved before extensive sorting or reorganization.

The preferred first phase is:

**inventory → photograph/scan → preserve original sequence → transcribe → classify → deduplicate**

Each notebook should receive a stable identifier.

Each page should be digitized in its existing order before sheets are separated or reorganized.

The digital corpus should preserve:

* notebook identity;
* page order;
* page images;
* transcription;
* annotations;
* original physical context;
* and relationships between duplicate sheets.

This allows later analysis without destroying the state in which the artifacts survived.

---

# Song-Level Reconstruction

Each unique song should eventually become a structured record.

Useful information includes:

* title;
* alternate title;
* lyric opening;
* complete surviving lyrics;
* chord progression;
* key, where available;
* capo information;
* artist attribution;
* songwriter;
* publisher;
* copyright information;
* approximate release date;
* notebook occurrence(s);
* ministry context;
* church-provided versus personally added status;
* confidence in that classification;
* annotations;
* duplicate count;
* identified historical recording or arrangement;
* recognition notes;
* performance evidence;
* setlist evidence;
* and source images.

Duplicate sheets should not simply be deleted.

Repeated occurrence can provide information about:

* repertoire persistence;
* cross-ministry use;
* changing arrangements;
* repeated distribution;
* different keys;
* and the relative centrality of particular songs.

Deduplication should therefore identify a canonical song record while preserving every physical occurrence.

---

# Historical Song Identification

Some sheets may contain an artist reference.

Others may contain only lyrics and chords.

Where identification is uncertain, songs may be reconstructed through combinations of:

* distinctive lyric phrases;
* chord sequences;
* copyright text;
* songwriter names;
* approximate date;
* worship-song databases;
* period recordings;
* church-music catalogs;
* and other historical sources.

Once identified, the corpus can associate the physical artifact with relevant historical information such as:

* original artist;
* songwriter;
* release year;
* album;
* worship movement;
* known arrangement;
* publisher;
* theological themes;
* and period recording.

The historical recording should remain distinct from the local chord sheet. The church may have used a different arrangement.

---

# Sonic Reconstruction Layer

Arm IV can go beyond textual cataloging because the surviving chord sheets encode enough musical information to support **synthetic sonic reconstruction**.

Using the chord progressions, lyrics, known keys, song structure, artist references, and period-style constraints, it is possible to generate plausible audio realizations of lost worship material.

My existing demo-song-generation workflow and tools such as Suno may be useful for this process.

The purpose is not to fabricate an archival recording.

The purpose is to create an **auditory model of the sonic possibilities encoded by the surviving artifact**.

This introduces a new evidentiary category:

## Synthetic Sonic Reconstruction

A newly generated recording based upon surviving musical evidence and explicitly documented assumptions.

It must always be labeled as reconstructed.

It must never be represented as:

* an original Calvary Spokane recording;
* the exact arrangement we played;
* proof of a historical performance;
* or a recovered historical audio artifact.

---

# Reconstruction Confidence

Synthetic reconstructions may vary in evidentiary constraint.

### High-Constraint Reconstruction

Possible when the artifact provides:

* lyrics;
* chord sequence;
* song identity;
* artist/reference recording;
* key;
* form;
* and other arrangement information.

The reconstruction can closely model a known historical version while remaining explicitly synthetic.

### Medium-Constraint Reconstruction

Possible when:

* song identity is known;
* chords and lyrics survive;
* but the exact Calvary arrangement is unknown.

A historically plausible worship arrangement can be modeled.

### Low-Constraint Reconstruction

Used when only partial chords, lyrics, or structural clues survive.

Multiple possible renderings may be appropriate.

These should be described as exploratory sonic models rather than historical reconstructions.

---

# Multiple Sonic Realizations

Where the local arrangement is unknown, generating more than one plausible version may be analytically useful.

For example:

* acoustic youth-group arrangement;
* full-band contemporary worship arrangement;
* stripped congregational arrangement;
* period-appropriate Christian rock arrangement.

The range of generated possibilities can show what the surviving chord sheet permits without falsely selecting one arrangement as historical fact.

Additional evidence may later narrow that range.

---

# Reconstructing a Sonic Environment

At sufficient scale, Arm IV could produce something more interesting than individual song recreations.

The reconstructed repertoire could support a **partial sonic model of the Calvary Spokane worship environment**.

This could include:

* common chord vocabulary;
* recurring keys;
* song tempo distribution;
* musical style;
* lyrical themes;
* emotional arcs;
* youth versus adult repertoire;
* Sunday versus Thursday differences;
* repeated composers;
* dominant worship movements or publishers;
* changes over time;
* and relationships between musical form and theological content.

The result would be a reconstruction not merely of **what was said**, but of **what the environment felt and sounded like**.

---

# Formation Through Performance

Arm IV also documents a different form of participation than passive exposure.

By playing on stage, I participated in producing the environment for others.

This means the repertoire functioned simultaneously as:

* material I received;
* material I learned;
* material I rehearsed;
* material I embodied as a performer;
* and material I helped deliver to a congregation or youth audience.

This creates a distinctive formation pathway.

The question is no longer simply:

> What was I taught?

It also becomes:

> **What was I trained to help teach, normalize, emotionalize, and reproduce through music?**

That distinction is central to Arm IV.

---

# Relationship to the Other Reconstruction Arms

Arm IV should remain analytically distinct while interoperating with the other corpora.

## Arm I — Sermon / Known Exposure Corpus

Arm I reconstructs recorded teaching and known sermon exposure.

Arm IV can eventually compare worship repertoire with sermon themes from the same periods.

## Arm II — Broader Media Ecosystem

Arm II concerns Calvary Spokane's larger media-production and distribution environment.

Some worship recordings may eventually surface there, but Arm IV does not depend on their existence.

## Arm III — Childhood and Adolescent Formation Environment

Arm III reconstructs the broader developmental environment of children's and youth programming.

The high-school youth worship material overlaps historically with Arm III, but Arm IV isolates worship because it constitutes a distinct medium and because my role involved active performance and transmission.

---

# Evidentiary Discipline

Arm IV should always separate:

* original physical artifact;
* artifact transcription;
* song identification;
* church-provided repertoire;
* personally added material;
* autobiographical recognition;
* performance evidence;
* setlist evidence;
* historical reference recording;
* analytical interpretation;
* and synthetic sonic reconstruction.

No synthetic output should overwrite or obscure the provenance of the original artifact.

The original notebook remains the primary source.

---

# Long-Term Analytical Possibilities

Once sufficiently reconstructed, Arm IV may allow analysis of:

* theological themes in worship lyrics;
* concepts of God and divine authority;
* salvation language;
* surrender and obedience;
* intimacy and emotional dependency;
* warfare imagery;
* purity;
* identity;
* eschatology;
* concepts of truth;
* social belonging;
* emotional regulation through worship;
* repetition and memorization;
* musical build-and-release patterns;
* repertoire differences by ministry population;
* relationships between worship themes and sermon themes;
* and changes in the church's musical/theological environment over time.

Interpretive analysis should follow corpus construction rather than precede it.

---

# Core Principle

Arm IV is not an attempt to manufacture a lost past.

It is an attempt to reconstruct as much of that past as surviving evidence permits while clearly marking uncertainty.

The physical notebooks provide the historical anchor.

Public archival research can identify the songs and their original context.

Personal memory can contribute recognition and performance evidence.

Synthetic intelligence and music-generation systems can then transform the surviving musical structure into **explicitly hypothetical sonic realizations**.

Together, these methods may allow a significant portion of an otherwise lost worship environment to become inspectable again.

# Arm IV

**Worship Repertoire, Performance, and Sonic Reconstruction**

> **Reconstructing the music I was given, learned, performed, and helped transmit—and, where the historical audio is lost, building transparent sonic models of what those surviving musical artifacts could have sounded like.**
